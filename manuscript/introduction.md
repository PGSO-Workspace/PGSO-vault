Voice agents can receive information about how an utterance is delivered as well as what it says. The engineering question is how that information should influence the interaction when its interpretation is uncertain. Affective systems already connect perception to behavior: Gao et al. combine multimodal emotion recognition, dialogue generation, and physical expression in a social robot, while Saffaryazdi et al. study adaptive interaction using neural and physiological observations [@gao2024multimodal; @saffaryazdi2025empathetic]. These precedents motivate context-sensitive interaction but do not establish that every inferred affective state warrants an intervention. For a tool-augmented agent, an inappropriate intervention can also affect which operations remain available to pursue the user's task.

Speech-derived affect is an uncertain input to such decisions. ParaLBench compares acoustic representations across paralinguistic tasks, and work on cross-corpus adaptation examines how recognition changes between corpora and recording conditions [@zhang2024paralbench; @ahn2025multitask]. Noise-robust speech emotion recognition further shows why evaluation must consider acoustic conditions outside the development setting [@leem2026describe]. Temporal interpretation introduces another difficulty: continuous and sentence-level annotations are not interchangeable, and their aggregation and alignment influence the resulting recognition problem [@martinezlucas2024analyzing]. A measured acoustic deviation should therefore not be equated with a verified emotional state, consent decision, or intention to perform a tool action.

Existing work also distinguishes the perception problem from the choice of response. Mallol-Ragolta and Schuller connect sentiment and arousal estimates to an affective dialogue manager, providing a concrete precedent for using inferred affect in response selection [@mallolragolta2024coupling]. At the language-model level, structured context can change expressed personality traits, and context manipulations can alter questionnaire-like indicators of model state [@serapiogarcia2025psychometric; @benzion2025assessing]. These findings concern expressed behavior under specific prompting and measurement conditions. They neither imply that a model possesses human psychological states nor show that changing its tool catalog induces a stable personality. They do, however, make a context-informed reference agent a necessary comparator for isolating the contribution of additional information: supplying extra observations may itself change behavior before an external orchestration mechanism contributes anything.

Tool use adds an execution boundary to this problem. Architectural work such as LLM-Agent-UMF distinguishes model, tool, and agent components and discusses security responsibilities within the overall system [@hassouna2026llmagentumf]. For the present study, prompt instructions and invocation checks are distinct architectural interventions. AgentSpec provides a directly implemented precedent for external rule enforcement in language-model agents [@wang2026agentspec]. Conversely, an execution check does not determine whether an observation-conditioned restriction was useful. The design must distinguish the host's authorization policy, the adaptive interaction policy, and the evidence used to evaluate each. An agent can remain within its permitted operations while failing to complete a legitimate task.

Uncertainty-aware prediction and runtime shielding provide complementary methodological lessons. Selective conformal prediction makes selection and coverage explicit under stated assumptions, while set-valued uncertainty methods examine the relation between calibration and the size of the prediction region [@bao2025general; @lutzow2025zonoconformal]. Those guarantees cannot be transferred directly to an uncalibrated acoustic heuristic or to the appropriateness of an intervention. Similarly, permissive runtime shielding studies the balance between enforcing constraints and preserving the behavior of a controller [@shi2026synthesizing]. Together these lines of work motivate evaluating retained capability alongside restrictions, rather than treating frequent abstention or blocking as evidence of success.

This study investigates Paralinguistic Governance for State Orchestration (PGSO), an experimental software mechanism that connects timestamped acoustic observations to explicit intervention state. Its design separates observation extraction, temporal decision logic, policy actions, and tool invocation. The proposed contribution is the integration and evaluation of reversible context directives and tool restrictions under uncertain observations, including their persistence, retirement, and execution traceability. It is not the general invention of affective interaction or deterministic runtime enforcement.

The primary engineering question is to what extent PGSO provides an inspectable and reusable observation-to-intervention lifecycle, preserves configured execution invariants, and supports recovery with measurable operational cost. A secondary research question is whether PGSO improves independently assessed task outcomes relative to an equally informed context-based agent while retaining legitimate actions and controlling executed policy violations. Three objectives follow: formalize the observation-to-intervention lifecycle and its assumptions; implement a reusable execution path whose effects can be inspected independently; and compare conformance and operational behavior under matched policy workloads, with informed prompting and simpler temporal controls retained for a secondary task-utility assessment. The evaluation distinguishes software contract checks from task utility and treats unfavorable effects as evidence about the design. Public simulated voice tasks provide the proposed application setting for this paper; company-specific sales conversion is reserved for a separate field study.

Section 2 reviews affective perception, adaptive action, tool-surface governance, and abstention and enforcement. Section 3 describes the design-science methodology, and Section 4 presents the mechanism and architecture. Section 5 reports the evaluation evidence, Section 6 interprets its scope and limitations, and Section 7 concludes the study.

# 2. Related Work

## 2.1 Affective speech perception

Recent speech-affect research treats the choice of representation, evaluation corpus, annotation protocol, and acoustic environment as separate determinants of recognition performance. ParaLBench compares acoustic foundation-model representations across computational paralinguistic tasks, providing a basis for selecting and comparing perception components [@zhang2024paralbench]. Such a benchmark does not establish the appropriateness of a downstream intervention: a representation that improves an emotion metric need not improve tool-mediated task completion.

Ahn et al. investigate cross-corpus recognition through a multitask transformer [@ahn2025multitask]. Their adaptation setting is relevant to distribution differences, but must not be interpreted as evidence of reliable zero-shot transfer to unseen speakers or deployments. Martinez-Lucas et al. examine continuous-time and sentence-level emotion annotations [@martinezlucas2024analyzing]. Their distinction matters for a temporal governor: an utterance-level label is not a ground-truth label for each overlapping acoustic window, and annotation alignment affects what persistence can mean operationally.

Leem et al. incorporate descriptions of the acoustic environment to improve noise-robust recognition [@leem2026describe]. The study motivates explicit degraded-speech conditions and checks with incorrect contextual information. It does not establish robustness to arbitrary noise or justify assuming that a supplied environmental description is correct. Together these works motivate a replaceable perception interface and reporting of uncertainty, transfer, and timing. PGSO's contribution must be evaluated downstream of that interface rather than inferred from an extractor ranking.

## 2.2 Affective perception and agent action

The general connection between affective perception and adaptive behavior predates PGSO. Mallol-Ragolta and Schuller couple sentiment and arousal analysis to an affective dialogue manager [@mallolragolta2024coupling]. This provides a direct precedent for observation-conditioned response selection. Their inspected conclusion identifies comparative dialogue-manager and real-user evaluation as further work, so the architectural connection should not be presented as demonstrated interaction superiority.

Gao et al. combine multimodal perception, dialogue generation, and affective physical expression in a hierarchical social-robot framework [@gao2024multimodal]. The resulting action space includes embodied expression, illustrating that inferred affect can influence more than generated text. That embodiment differs from permission-governed software operations. The relevance to PGSO is the perception-to-behavior architecture; its robot interaction measurements cannot be substituted for tool-task outcomes.

Saffaryazdi et al. study adaptive conversational interaction using neural and physiological signals [@saffaryazdi2025empathetic]. Their human-interaction setting supplies a precedent for evaluating experienced interaction quality, while the sensing modality and avatar-based treatment differ from a prosodic tool governor. Jamil et al. instead condition a response generator on multimodal emotion representations and evaluate emotion recognition and generated responses on benchmark corpora [@jamil2026eacagent]. These are useful perception-to-response comparisons, but offline recognition and language metrics do not establish live task utility or authorized execution.

Amaral et al. describe an architecture connecting user-monitoring inputs, a knowledge graph, and empathy-aware behavior trees that select actions or suggestions [@amaral2025combining]. The institutional abstract presents a driver–copilot example and an early implementation, mainly in Prolog. This is a close architectural precedent for affect-conditioned action; the accessible abstract does not establish invocation-level authorization or comparative task benefit. The full article was unavailable for independent inspection. PGSO must therefore distinguish its implemented intervention lifecycle and measured consequences from the broader idea of connecting affective sensing to action.

Cohn et al. use a multimodal timeline to support interpretation of student difficulty and decisions about when to intervene [@cohn2024multimodal]. Their distinction between a difficulty threshold and a later intervention point is relevant to temporal action selection. The inspected study examines researcher–teacher collaboration in two student-group scenarios, with intervention remaining teacher-mediated. It supplies a precedent for separating detection from intervention timing, while leaving autonomous tool dispatch and authorization outside its evaluation.

Together, these approaches address adaptive behavior through response selection, generation, expression, or mediated intervention. PGSO studies the additional problem of maintaining and retiring an explicit intervention state that also reaches a tool-dispatch boundary. Whether this addition helps remains an empirical question. It cannot be justified by claiming that previous agents ignored affect or that emotionally conditioned text alone guarantees an appropriate action.

## 2.3 Governance through the tool surface and induced personality

Context-conditioned behavior and runtime authority are related but distinct. Serapio-García et al. evaluate and shape expressed personality traits in language models through structured prompts [@serapiogarcia2025psychometric]. Results vary across configurations, supporting an operational interpretation of measured behavior rather than an assumption of a stable human personality. Ben-Zion et al. examine questionnaire-like state-anxiety outputs under narrative and relaxation contexts [@benzion2025assessing]. Their single-model measurement setting offers adjacent evidence that context changes outputs; it does not show experienced anxiety, tool competence, or safer execution.

Li and Qi investigate language-model responses to personality and cultural variables [@li2025toward]. The accessible publisher summary reports limits in reproducing human psychological and cultural patterns. This source is used here only for that stated scope; its full methods and results were not available for independent examination. Consequently, none of these studies is treated as evidence that pruning a tool catalog induces a stable personality or improves a task.

Hassouna et al. propose an architectural modeling framework that separates models, tools, coordinating agents, and security responsibilities [@hassouna2026llmagentumf]. The inspected author manuscript evaluates architectural scenarios rather than executed implementations of those combinations. It nevertheless supplies relevant architectural context for distinguishing a profile or instruction component from an action-monitoring component. PGSO's invocation boundary must be assessed as an implemented path under explicit host assumptions, not justified solely by an architectural diagram.

For the present study, a removable directive changes available context, while catalog and dispatch policies constrain accessible operations. These interventions may change behavior, but their behavioral effect and their enforcement effect require separate measurements. An informed prompting comparator is therefore included alongside deterministic alternatives. The historical subsection title is retained, but induced personality is not adopted as an established PGSO outcome or a proxy for utility.

## 2.4 Abstention, enforcement, and runtime safety

Abstention changes the population on which a predictor is evaluated. Bao et al.'s CAP framework explicitly studies online selection and false coverage-statement control under stated assumptions [@bao2025general]. Its finite-sample and adaptive results have different conditions, and its access to prior outcomes is consequential. These guarantees cannot be assigned to a voicing-quality heuristic that has neither corresponding calibration labels nor the required assumptions.

Lützow et al. study zonotope-based uncertainty sets for regression and classification, examining coverage and the size of the resulting sets [@lutzow2025zonoconformal]. Their work motivates reporting both uncertainty coverage and retained informativeness. Jia et al. apply calibrated prediction sets to speech emotion recognition [@jia2025coverageguaranteed]. The inspected author version evaluates emotion prediction on acted corpora; the journal version has not been compared line by line. Prediction-set coverage remains distinct from the correctness of a policy selected from that set, particularly under a different deployment distribution.

Shi et al. synthesize efficient and permissive runtime shields for neural policies [@shi2026synthesizing]. This is a methodological precedent for evaluating constraint enforcement alongside how much valid behavior the controller retains. Its control-system formulation and assumptions do not transfer directly to conversational tools, but the distinction prevents a refusal-only system from being judged successful merely because it executes no violating action.

AgentSpec offers a closer tool-agent comparison than controller shielding: its event-triggered rules intervene before actions, after observations, and at completion through agent-framework hooks [@wang2026agentspec]. Its evaluated enforcement and retained-task tradeoffs make external action control and rule-triggered interventions prior art. The study uses authored rules and includes manual outcome judgments; it does not predict every future unsafe trajectory. We include this ICSE conference paper as a methodological comparator, separately from the recent-Q1 journal quota. PGSO must be compared with an equally informed AgentSpec integration before claiming an implementation-specific advantage over this mechanism.

PGSO combines an abstention policy with temporal intervention state and an invocation check. Abstention in the inspected implementation preserves prior state; it is not automatically a return to nominal operation. Similarly, preventing an unauthorized callback is a property of the controlled execution path, whereas preventing an inappropriate restriction is a property of the observation-conditioned interaction policy. The evaluation must expose both failure modes. Neither conformal prediction nor runtime enforcement independently resolves their combination.

## 2.5 Synthesis of the gap

The reviewed literature supplies precedents for affect recognition, affect-conditioned responses, prompt-shaped behavior, uncertainty-aware selection, and external action control. The research opportunity is consequently narrower than any of those components: an explicit and inspectable lifecycle from uncertain acoustic observations to reversible context and tool interventions, evaluated against equally informed and operationally capable alternatives.

Table I summarizes the related-work families and their implications for evaluation.

**TABLE I. Related-work synthesis and questions requiring separate evidence**

| Literature family | Existing contribution relevant to PGSO | Question requiring separate evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Affective perception | Representations, temporal annotation, adaptation, and noise robustness | Does an observation improve the subsequent action decision? |
| Affective agent action | Response selection, generation, and embodied adaptation | Does adding tool intervention retain or improve task completion? |
| Context and tool architecture | Expressed behavior under prompts and separation of agent responsibilities | What benefit remains over an equally informed prompting agent? |
| Uncertainty and runtime control | Calibrated prediction sets and permissive enforcement | Is a restriction both correctly executed and appropriate for the task? |

This mapping defines the comparisons required by the study; it is not proof that an unoccupied novelty category exists. The review is targeted, with source versions and access limits retained in the research record. A first-ever claim would require a stronger search and comparison argument. PGSO's proposed contribution instead depends on reproducible lifecycle semantics and independently measured consequences for permitted task execution.
