Voice-agent adaptation requires more than detecting acoustic variation: uncertain observations must be translated into interventions whose execution and consequences can be inspected. We present a paralinguistic governance layer that maintains temporal intervention state, composes removable directives and tool restrictions, and checks host-controlled authorization at invocation. The design separates acoustic assessment, policy composition, and actual tool effects. Freezing baseline adaptation during sustained deviations prevents those deviations from being absorbed. Explicit retirement supports policy recovery, while approvals bound to the policy revision govern execution that requires additional authorization. The available evaluation combines acoustic intensity diagnostics, matched operational workloads, and integration with public simulated telecom tasks. These checks exercise persistence, restoration, and the execution boundary, but do not establish conversational benefit. An additional sandbox diagnostic exposes a consequential limitation: sustained injected observations can withhold a legitimate repair even when the runtime enforces the configured policy correctly. The resulting distinction between enforcement conformance and intervention appropriateness motivates independent task-outcome evaluation against equally informed contextual adaptation and instantaneous intervention. Task utility, unnecessary restriction, and recovery must be assessed separately. A real-generation pilot across seven configurations reached a common task-failure floor. Comparative interaction benefits remain unresolved; the present evidence supports an inspectable software mechanism and identifies a treatment risk, rather than demonstrating superior empathy or task performance.
